# Hierarchy Problem

> [!summary] Core idea
> The electroweak scale is radiatively sensitive to much higher scales in simple formulations, motivating but not proving new physics.

## Physical content

The hierarchy problem is the question of why the electroweak scale $v \approx 246$ GeV (the Higgs vacuum expectation value, which sets the masses of the $W$ and $Z$ bosons) is so vastly smaller than the Planck scale $M_{\rm Pl} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$ GeV -- a ratio of roughly $10^{-17}$, or equivalently 17 orders of magnitude. In the [[Standard Model]], nothing forbids the Higgs boson mass from receiving quantum corrections that are quadratically sensitive to whatever ultraviolet scale the Standard Model is embedded in. If that scale is the Planck scale, then the "natural" value of the Higgs mass would be $\sim M_{\rm Pl}$, and the observed value $m_H \approx 125$ GeV requires a spectacular cancellation between the bare mass and quantum corrections.

More concretely, consider a one-loop correction to the Higgs mass from a fermion $f$ with Yukawa coupling $y_f$ to the Higgs. This yields a correction:

$$\delta m_H^2 = -\frac{|y_f|^2}{8\pi^2} \Lambda_{\rm UV}^2 + \ldots$$

where $\Lambda_{\rm UV}$ is the ultraviolet cutoff (the scale up to which the Standard Model is valid as an effective field theory). If $\Lambda_{\rm UV} \sim M_{\rm Pl}$, then $\delta m_H^2 \sim 10^{30}\ \text{GeV}^2$, while the physical Higgs mass-squared is $m_H^2 \sim 10^4\ \text{GeV}^2$. Achieving the physical value requires the bare mass parameter to cancel this radiative correction to one part in $10^{26}$. This is the **fine-tuning** at the heart of the hierarchy problem.

It is important to distinguish the hierarchy problem from a mere numerological coincidence. The issue is one of **radiative instability** (also called **technical naturalness**, following 't Hooft): a small parameter is "technically natural" if setting it to zero increases the symmetry of the theory. The electron mass is technically natural because $m_e \to 0$ restores chiral symmetry, which protects $m_e$ from large corrections (the corrections are proportional to $m_e$ itself). The Higgs mass is a scalar mass, and setting $m_H = 0$ does not restore any symmetry of the Standard Model. Thus the Higgs mass has no protective symmetry, and its smallness is radiatively unstable -- loop corrections drag it toward the UV cutoff.

Several classes of proposed solutions have been explored:

**Supersymmetry (SUSY)**: Each Standard Model particle has a superpartner with spin differing by $1/2$. The quadratic divergence from a fermion loop is exactly cancelled by the corresponding sfermion loop, because bosonic and fermionic loops contribute with opposite signs. If SUSY were exact, the cancellation would be perfect; since SUSY must be broken (superpartners have not been observed), the correction becomes $\delta m_H^2 \sim (m_{\tilde{f}}^2 - m_f^2)/(16\pi^2)$, which is acceptably small if superpartner masses are not too far above the TeV scale. The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model (MSSM) was for decades the leading candidate for beyond-Standard-Model physics.

**Compositeness / Technicolor**: The Higgs is not an elementary scalar but a bound state of new strong dynamics at a scale $\Lambda \sim$ TeV. Just as the pion in QCD is a pseudo-Goldstone boson of chiral symmetry breaking, the Higgs could be a pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone boson of a new symmetry broken at a few TeV. This naturally explains why $m_H \ll M_{\rm Pl}$ without fine-tuning: the Higgs mass is generated by explicit symmetry breaking and is parametrically lighter than the compositeness scale.

**Extra dimensions**: In the ADD (Arkani-Hamed, Dimopoulos, Dvali, 1998) scenario, gravity propagates in $n$ large extra dimensions of radius $R$, diluting its strength. The fundamental gravity scale $M_*$ can be as low as a few TeV, with the apparent weakness of gravity ($M_{\rm Pl}^2 \sim M_*^{n+2} R^n$) explained by the large volume of the extra dimensions. In the Randall-Sundrum (RS, 1999) model, a single warped extra dimension with an exponential "warp factor" generates the hierarchy from an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ ratio of length scales: the Planck scale at one "brane" is exponentially redshifted to the TeV scale at the other.

**Relaxion mechanism** (Graham, Kaplan, Rajendran, 2015): A cosmological relaxation mechanism in which an axion-like field slowly rolls during inflation, dynamically scanning the Higgs mass parameter until electroweak symmetry breaking triggers a backreaction potential that stops the rolling.

**Anthropic / landscape reasoning**: In the string landscape, the enormous number of vacua ($\sim 10^{500}$) each with different low-energy parameters might explain fine-tuning via environmental selection: we observe a small Higgs mass because only vacua with $m_H \sim v$ produce atoms, chemistry, and observers. This approach is philosophically contentious and essentially gives up on a dynamical explanation.

## Mathematical framework

The **one-loop Higgs mass correction** from a Dirac fermion with Yukawa coupling $y_f$ is:

$$\delta m_H^2 = -\frac{N_c |y_f|^2}{8\pi^2} \left[\Lambda_{\rm UV}^2 - 6 m_f^2 \ln\left(\frac{\Lambda_{\rm UV}}{m_f}\right) + \ldots\right]$$

where $N_c$ is the number of colors (3 for quarks, 1 for leptons). The leading $\Lambda_{\rm UV}^2$ term is the quadratic divergence. In dimensional regularization, quadratic divergences are set to zero by convention, but this does not eliminate the physical sensitivity: matching to a UV completion at scale $\Lambda$ reintroduces the $\Lambda^2$ dependence as a threshold correction.

The scalar loop from a boson with quartic coupling $\lambda$ gives:

$$\delta m_H^2 = +\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2} \left[\Lambda_{\rm UV}^2 - 2 m_S^2 \ln\left(\frac{\Lambda_{\rm UV}}{m_S}\right) + \ldots\right]$$

Note the opposite sign relative to the fermion loop -- this is the basis of the SUSY cancellation.

In **SUSY**, with a superpartner mass $m_{\tilde{f}}$, the combined correction becomes:

$$\delta m_H^2 \sim \frac{|y_f|^2}{16\pi^2} (m_{\tilde{f}}^2 - m_f^2) \ln\left(\frac{\Lambda_{\rm UV}}{m_{\tilde{f}}}\right)$$

The quadratic divergence has cancelled, leaving a correction proportional to the SUSY-breaking mass splitting. For the hierarchy to be stable, one needs $m_{\tilde{f}} \lesssim$ few TeV.

The **fine-tuning measure** (Barbieri-Giudice, 1988) quantifies how sensitively the $Z$-boson mass depends on fundamental parameters:

$$\Delta_{a} = \left|\frac{\partial \ln m_Z^2}{\partial \ln a}\right|$$

where $a$ ranges over all fundamental parameters. A theory with $\Delta \gg 1$ is considered fine-tuned. The Standard Model with $\Lambda_{\rm UV} = M_{\rm Pl}$ has $\Delta \sim 10^{26}$.

## Key results and implications

- The hierarchy problem is the primary theoretical motivation for new physics at the TeV scale. It drove the design energy of the LHC.
- The discovery of the Higgs boson at 125 GeV (ATLAS and CMS, 2012) confirmed the existence of an elementary scalar but found no accompanying new particles. This value is tantalizingly close to the upper bound predicted by the MSSM ($\lesssim 135$ GeV) but is achieved only in regions of parameter space that themselves require some tuning.
- LHC Run 1 and Run 2 (2010-2018) found **no evidence for supersymmetry, compositeness, or extra dimensions** at the TeV scale. Gluino masses are excluded below $\sim 2.3$ TeV, stop masses below $\sim 1.3$ TeV, and contact interactions are constrained above $\sim 30$ TeV. This has precipitated what some call a **"naturalness crisis"** in particle physics.
- The hierarchy problem is conceptually linked to the [[Cosmological Constant Problem]]: both involve the radiative instability of a dimensionful parameter. The cosmological constant problem is even more severe ($\sim 10^{120}$ rather than $\sim 10^{26}$).
- Some physicists (e.g., Wilson, 2004) have argued that the hierarchy problem may reflect a misunderstanding of how effective field theories relate to their UV completions, rather than a genuine need for new physics.

## Failure modes and limitations

- A common misconception is treating the hierarchy problem as a logical inconsistency in the Standard Model. The Standard Model is perfectly consistent with a finely-tuned Higgs mass; the hierarchy problem is an aesthetic/theoretical concern about naturalness, not a mathematical contradiction.
- In dimensional regularization, the quadratic divergence $\Lambda^2$ does not appear, leading some to argue the problem is an artifact of the regularization scheme. However, the physical content reappears as threshold corrections when matching to any UV completion at a high scale.
- The absence of new physics at the LHC has sharpened the question of whether naturalness is a reliable guide to physics beyond the Standard Model. The history of physics provides examples where fine-tuning was resolved by new physics (the electron self-energy problem, resolved by the positron) and examples where it was not (the cosmological constant).
- If one takes the string landscape and anthropic reasoning seriously, the hierarchy problem may simply have no dynamical solution -- the electroweak scale is environmentally selected. This would represent a fundamental shift in the methodology of theoretical physics.

## Experimental evidence

- The Higgs boson discovery at $m_H = 125.1$ GeV (ATLAS and CMS, 2012) established that an elementary scalar exists, making the hierarchy problem a concrete, experimentally relevant question rather than a hypothetical one.
- LHC searches for SUSY, compositeness, and extra dimensions have yielded null results, pushing the natural parameter space of all proposed solutions to increasingly fine-tuned regions. Direct searches for superpartners now probe well above the TeV scale.
- Precision electroweak measurements (from LEP, SLD, Tevatron, and LHC) constrain oblique corrections and new-physics contributions at the loop level, further narrowing the viable parameter space for solutions to the hierarchy problem.
- Future colliders (proposed FCC-hh at 100 TeV, muon colliders) would extend the reach significantly, potentially covering the full natural parameter space for SUSY and composite Higgs models.
