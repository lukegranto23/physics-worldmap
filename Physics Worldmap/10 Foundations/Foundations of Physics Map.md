# Foundations of Physics Map

> [!abstract]
> The language and standards by which physical claims become meaningful, testable, limited, and connected.

## Questions that organize the field

- What makes a physical model explanatory rather than merely predictive?
- Which structures are fundamental, and which are emergent or effective?
- How do symmetry, causality, locality, probability, and measurement constrain theories?
- What distinguishes a solved problem from a useful approximation or an open interpretation?

## Working principles

- State observables, operational definitions, and units before interpreting symbols.
- Separate empirical facts, mathematical theorems, models, approximations, and metaphysical interpretations.
- Demand dimensional consistency, symmetry consistency, correct limiting behavior, and falsifiable consequences.
- Treat every theory as having a domain of validity and an uncertainty budget.

## Canonical mathematical handles

- **Dimensional homogeneity:** $[LHS]=[RHS]$ — Every valid physical equation equates like dimensions.
- **Action principle:** $\delta S=0$ — Dynamics often follow from stationary action.
- **Noether correspondence:** $\text{continuous symmetry}\Rightarrow\text{conserved current}$ — Symmetry organizes laws and conserved quantities.
- **Bayes rule:** $P(H\mid D)=P(D\mid H)P(H)/P(D)$ — Evidence updates uncertainty about models.

| [[Observables and Operational Definitions]] | introductory | established | An observable must be tied to a reproducible measurement procedure, including calibration and uncertainty. |
| [[Units Dimensions and Conventions]] | introductory | established | Units encode measurement standards while dimensions expose invariant structure and constrain equations. |
| [[Dimensional Analysis]] | introductory | established | Buckingham Pi reasoning reduces a problem to independent dimensionless groups and predicts scaling before detailed dynamics. |
| [[Symmetry in Physics]] | intermediate | established | A symmetry is a transformation that preserves specified physical structure; it classifies states, interactions, and conservation laws. |
| [[Conservation Laws]] | introductory | established | Conserved quantities remain constant under evolution and usually reflect symmetry, constraints, or topological structure. |
| [[Locality]] | advanced | established | Local theories couple quantities at the same spacetime point or infinitesimally nearby points, while quantum correlations complicate naive intuitions. |
| [[Causality]] | intermediate | established | Causal structure specifies which events can influence others and constrains response functions, relativistic propagation, and inference. |
| [[Determinism and Predictability]] | introductory | established | Deterministic laws may still be practically unpredictable because of chaos, incomplete data, or computational complexity. |
| [[Probability in Physics]] | intermediate | established | Probability may represent ensembles, frequencies, propensities, typicality, or rational uncertainty depending on context. |
| [[Measurement and Calibration]] | introductory | established | Measurement is a physical interaction plus an inference pipeline that maps instrument response to a reported quantity. |
| [[Uncertainty and Error]] | introductory | established | Uncertainty quantifies incomplete knowledge; error is a realized deviation and may be statistical or systematic. |
| [[Falsifiability and Model Comparison]] | intermediate | established | Competing models are compared by risky predictions, goodness of fit, predictive performance, and complexity-aware evidence. |
| [[Correspondence Principle]] | introductory | established | A newer theory must reproduce successful older descriptions in the regime where those descriptions were tested. |
| [[Effective Theories]] | advanced | established | An effective theory keeps degrees of freedom relevant to a chosen scale and parameterizes unresolved physics in controlled operators. |
| [[Emergence]] | intermediate | active | Collective laws and entities can be robust and autonomous even when they arise from different microscopic realizations. |
| [[Reduction and Universality]] | advanced | established | Reduction derives higher-level behavior from lower-level rules; universality explains why many microscopically different systems share it. |
| [[Idealization and Approximation]] | introductory | established | Point particles, continua, isolated systems, and linear response are controlled simplifications whose errors require tracking. |
| [[Naturalness and Fine Tuning]] | advanced | open | Naturalness heuristics ask why dimensionless parameters or cancellations appear unusually small, but their evidential force is debated. |
| [[Scientific Realism and Interpretation]] | advanced | open | Interpretations ask what successful formal structures say exists; most do not change tested predictions. |
| [[Arrow of Time]] | intermediate | open | Microscopic laws are often nearly time-reversal invariant while macroscopic records and entropy production select a direction. |
| [[Initial and Boundary Conditions]] | intermediate | established | Laws alone rarely specify a world; initial states, boundary data, and selection principles complete predictions. |
| [[Physical Constants]] | introductory | established | Measured constants set conversion scales and interaction strengths; dimensionless combinations carry invariant physical content. |
| [[Theory Space]] | advanced | active | A theory space organizes possible models by fields, symmetries, parameters, and flows between scale-dependent descriptions. |

## Suggested study loop

2. Learn the introductory concepts and reproduce their limiting cases.
3. Derive at least one canonical equation from a variational, symmetry, or conservation principle.
4. Reproduce one landmark result computationally or from public data.
5. Audit one frontier claim: known facts, model dependence, discriminating observable, and next experiment.
